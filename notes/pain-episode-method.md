# Pain episode method

A broad opportunity is useful for deciding **where to look**. A pain episode is the unit for deciding **what to do next**.

## Atomic unit

A pain episode is a concrete situation with:

1. a specific actor
2. a detectable trigger
3. a job the actor is trying to complete
4. observable artifacts created by the work
5. a surface complaint
6. the actor's believed cause
7. an evidence-backed or explicitly inferred mechanism
8. a workflow and its broken handoffs
9. actors, incentives and decision power
10. time and money exposure
11. current spend and workarounds
12. an identifiable buyer and budget owner
13. a distribution path tied to the trigger
14. competitor coverage at the workflow-step level
15. a narrow intervention
16. a falsifiable paid experiment
17. a success condition
18. a kill criterion

The desired pattern is:

```text
pain -> mistaken explanation -> mechanism -> intervention -> experiment -> result
```

## Economics

Do not hide uncertainty behind a single fake-precise number. Store ranges when the data is approximate.

Useful episode-level measures include:

- time lost per episode
- frequency per year
- professional hourly value
- delay cost per day
- money directly at risk
- existing software, consultant and labor spend
- revenue or loss that the intervention can plausibly influence

Derived values can include annual time exposure and midpoint loss estimates, but the underlying assumptions must remain visible.

## Claim ledger

Every material claim is marked as one of:

- **measured**: directly supported by a source or observed dataset
- **reported**: a credible source reports the behavior or problem, but it is not our own measurement
- **inferred**: our interpretation of multiple observations
- **hypothesized**: a proposition that still needs a test

The UI must not make inferred mechanisms look like measured prevalence.

## Information gain

Opportunity score asks: **How attractive does this look now?**

Information Gain asks: **How much could the next realistic experiment change our decision?**

High information gain usually means:

- one accessible artifact could reveal the mechanism
- the current uncertainty is decision-critical
- a small sample can falsify a large assumption
- payment behavior can be tested before building software
- success and failure are observable quickly

A high current score with low information gain may deserve less immediate research than a slightly lower-scoring opportunity whose uncertainty can be resolved in days.

## Artifact-first research

Prefer real work artifacts over interviews alone.

Examples:

- status certificates and reserve studies
- contractor quotes and permit assumptions
- RFI and submittal histories
- prior-authorization packets and denials
- POS, labor and invoice exports
- AI prompts, outputs and review traces
- work orders and reopen histories
- client document request lists and chase threads

Interviews explain what people believe. Artifacts reveal what actually happened.

## Trigger-first distribution

For every pain episode, ask when the user becomes unusually motivated and whether that moment is detectable.

Examples:

- a condo status package arrives
- parental leave has 90 days remaining
- a contractor asks for a deposit
- a parent receives a hospital discharge date
- a client requests out-of-scope work
- a prior authorization is denied
- a container's free-time clock starts
- an AI-authored pull request enters review

The better the trigger, the easier it is to find customers without broad demographic targeting.

## Competitors

Do not stop at a list of companies. Map competitors and workarounds to the workflow steps they solve.

For each one record:

- what step it solves
- what it leaves unresolved
- who pays for it
- whether it moves the problem earlier or later
- whether users still bridge gaps with email, spreadsheets, phone calls or human coordinators

A valuable wedge often lives between existing products rather than in a completely empty market.

## Experiment standard

Every intervention-ready episode ends with:

```text
Who: exact actor and context
Trigger: event that makes the pain acute
Artifact: what we need to inspect
Offer: manual intervention before software
Price: real payment test where feasible
Sample: number and qualification criteria
Success: measurable outcome
Kill: condition that tells us to stop
Timebox: how long we permit the test to run
```

The kill criterion is mandatory. Research without a kill condition tends to rationalize weak ideas.

## Archetypes

Pain archetypes connect superficially different industries through the same mechanism. Current archetypes include:

- high-stakes document interpretation
- deadline-driven bureaucracy
- fragmented entitlement navigation
- multi-party coordination collapse
- scope and revenue leakage
- invisible operational leakage
- AI trust and governance gap
- exception overload

Archetypes are useful for transfer. If an intervention works in one domain, search for the same mechanism in another domain with better economics, distribution or founder fit.

## Counterfactual comparisons

Do not ask only which episode scores higher. Ask why the same mechanism behaves differently across contexts.

Compare:

- actor and buyer
- trigger strength
- frequency
- money at risk
- professional time value
- distribution access
- regulatory/trust burden
- current spend
- experiment cost
- speed to outcome

The goal is to discover where a mechanism has the best combination of pain, leverage and testability.

## Evidence ladder

```text
complaint < search < observed workaround < existing spend < pays us < measurable result < repeatable result
```

Once paid experiments exist, behavioral evidence should dominate desk-research scores.
